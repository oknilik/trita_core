## The same person, seen from different positions

We know things about ourselves that other people cannot see: our intentions, doubts, and the background to a situation. Colleagues, on the other hand, observe how we behave with them in everyday work. These two sources of information overlap, but they are not identical.

<Callout>
Self-report and observer report are not a true–false pair. They describe the same person from positions with access to different information.
</Callout>

Agreement depends on the trait being assessed, the questionnaire, the observer's relationship to the person, and how well they know each other. A single universal correlation therefore gives a misleadingly simple picture.

## A difference is not automatically a bias

A large meta-analysis found no general self-enhancement effect across personality traits. The direction and size of a difference can vary by dimension, relationship, and sample. It is therefore not defensible to claim that people generally overrate their <DimBadge code="C" label="Conscientiousness" />, or that a low <DimBadge code="H" label="Honesty–Humility" /> score implies self-enhancement.

In the standard HEXACO model, <DimBadge code="E" label="Emotionality" /> is also not simply the reverse of Big Five emotional stability. Interpretation must start from the actual content of the scale being used.

## Why can the two views differ?

**They cover different situations.** We know ourselves across many contexts; a colleague mainly rates the behaviour they have seen in shared work.

**Some traits are easier to observe.** Social signals of <DimBadge code="X" label="Extraversion" /> are relatively visible, while anxiety, intentions, and private deliberation are less accessible to an observer.

**Measurement is uncertain.** Item interpretation, response style, relationship quality, and the current context can all influence a score.

**An ideal self may appear in responses.** People may sometimes answer partly in line with who they want to be. This does not affect everyone, every trait, or every situation in the same direction.

## What do two perspectives add?

<KeyInsight>
A gap is not a verdict. It is a signal that needs interpretation: where does it appear, and what concrete behavioural evidence sits behind it?
</KeyInsight>

Observer feedback adds value when it is not used for labelling. A larger difference can open useful questions: do we see each other in different situations? Are expectations unclear? Is there a contribution that feels important internally but is less visible to others?

## How trita uses the comparison

trita does not decide who is right or describe either perspective as the more “realistic” one. It presents the difference as a prompt for discussion. At team level, interpretation requires concrete examples, multiple sources of evidence, and – in sensitive situations – a trained facilitator.
