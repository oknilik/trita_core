## The same person, from two vantage points

We know things about ourselves that nobody else can see: our intentions, our doubts, the background to a situation. Colleagues, in turn, see the part of this that shows up in shared work. Both pictures are real – they are just taken from different positions.

<Callout>
Self-report and colleague feedback do not compete. They show the same person from two vantage points, and together they see more than either does alone.
</Callout>

This is not just a nice idea – it is a measurable gain. Research shows that ratings from colleagues who know someone well track workplace behaviour at least as closely as self-report does for some traits. Relying on self-assessment alone means using half of the available picture.

## The difference is the most valuable signal

When the two pictures line up, that is confirmation: what someone believes about themselves is what others experience. The most useful conversations, however, start where the pictures differ. Three typical patterns:

**Hidden strength.** Colleagues rate someone's calm or reliability higher than they rate it themselves. Think of the developer who feels they are “just doing their job” – while half the team turns to them whenever something breaks. Spoken out loud, this becomes a resource; unspoken, it stays invisible.

**Blind spot.** Someone sees themselves as quick to respond, while the team waits days for their answers. Neither side is lying: one knows their intentions, the other knows their experience. The gap shows exactly what is worth talking about.

**Different situations.** A manager sees a team member in meetings; the person also knows their own deep work. The two pictures can differ while both are accurate – they simply describe different situations.

## Why can the two views differ?

The gap has everyday causes, even without any bias:

- **We see different situations.** We know ourselves across many contexts; a colleague mainly knows the shared work.
- **Not everything is visible from outside.** Social signals of <DimBadge code="X" label="Extraversion" /> are easy to observe; private deliberation or anxiety much less so.
- **Measurement always carries uncertainty.** How items are read, and what is going on in someone's life, both shape the answers.
- **An ideal self may play a part.** Sometimes we partly describe the person we would like to be.

## Three questions for your next conversation

<KeyInsight>
A gap is not a verdict – it is a conversation starter. Don't ask “who sees it right”; ask “what do we see differently, and why”.
</KeyInsight>

When a comparison shows a larger gap, these three questions almost always move things forward:

1. **“In which situations do we see each other?”** The picture may cover only meetings, while most of the work happens elsewhere.
2. **“What exactly do we expect from each other?”** On the fast–thorough or independent–aligned scale, differing expectations often show up as differing ratings.
3. **“Is there a contribution of yours you feel we don't see?”** This is where hidden strengths tend to surface.

## Sources and limitations

A gap on its own proves neither bias nor change over time: large-sample research found no general self-enhancement, and the direction of a difference varies by trait and relationship. A small score difference can sit within measurement uncertainty. That is why gaps should always be interpreted together with concrete examples – neither perspective is meant for labelling.
