## Self-assessment isn't dishonest – it's biased

When you complete a personality assessment, you probably believe you know yourself. Most people do. The problem is that there is a systematic gap between self-knowledge and the behaviour others actually see – and that gap is not random.

<Callout>
Self-assessment is not a lie. It is an inside perspective from which certain things are simply invisible.
</Callout>

Psychological research finds an average correlation of around r=0.4 between self-image and observer ratings. That means the two measures are related – but far from identical. And the difference is not arbitrary: some dimensions are systematically rated higher by the self than by anyone else.

## Which dimension is distorted the most?

<DimBadge code="C" label="Conscientiousness" /> is the most commonly overrated dimension. Everyone believes they are organised and reliable. The reality: in 68% of cases, outside observers give a lower C score than the person would give themselves.

<DimBadge code="H" label="Honesty-Humility" /> is distorted too – in the opposite way. People tend to believe they are ethical and straightforward. Those with genuinely low H scores overrate themselves on this dimension the most extremely.

<DimBadge code="E" label="Emotional stability" /> is another dimension where self-image usually looks better than what others experience. We see our own emotional reactions in context – others only see the behaviour.

## Why does self-assessment distort?

Three mechanisms operate at the same time:

**Self-serving bias** – a natural tendency to form a more positive picture of ourselves than reality warrants. This used to be evolutionarily adaptive (confidence meant better social standing), but today it adds noise to measurement.

**Contextual blindness** – we always interpret our own behaviour in context. "I was late because of the traffic." Others only see the lateness. This effect is especially strong on the <DimBadge code="A" label="Agreeableness" /> and <DimBadge code="E" label="Emotional stability" /> dimensions.

**Aspirational self-image** – we report the person we want to be, not the person we are. Someone with a low C score who values being organised will likely rate their own C higher than their behaviour shows.

## What does observer feedback solve?

The observer doesn't replace self-assessment – it complements it. The two perspectives together give a more accurate picture than either one alone.

<KeyInsight>
The gap itself is the data. Wherever self-image and the outside view differ substantially, it's worth looking deeper – not because someone lied, but because two different realities are being seen.
</KeyInsight>

Research shows that observer ratings are especially reliable on the <DimBadge code="X" label="Extraversion" /> and <DimBadge code="C" label="Conscientiousness" /> dimensions – the traits that are most visible in behaviour. For <DimBadge code="H" label="Honesty-Humility" /> and <DimBadge code="E" label="Emotional stability" />, self-assessment may offer more inside access.

## The observer feature in trita

In the trita system, observer feedback is not a verdict – it is a second perspective. The comparison doesn't show who sees things correctly; it shows where the two viewpoints diverge.

That divergence – once you understand why it's there – is more valuable than any single number on its own.
