A personality profile does not reveal who is going to resign. Zimmerman's 2008 meta-analysis did find statistical links between a few personality traits and actual turnover, but they are moderate. That is not enough for individual prediction. What the profile is good for is picking the topics of a developmental conversation.

## Leaving is never the result of a single cause

In 2017 Hom and colleagues reviewed a century of research on employee turnover. The overall picture is that departures are shaped together by personal, job-related, managerial, organisational, and labour-market factors. The relationship with one's manager is an important factor. The often-quoted claim that people generally leave their manager is, however, misleading.

In Gallup's 2024 US analysis, 42 percent of leavers said afterwards that their exit could have been prevented. That is retrospective self-report. It is not the same as having been able to say in advance who would leave. Confusing the two is the most common mistake in retention projects.

<Callout>
A personality profile is not an early warning system. It offers a perspective for a conversation, and we promise nothing more from it.
</Callout>

## The cost of replacement differs by organisation

The cost of a replacement includes recruitment, selection, onboarding, the time the role stays unfilled, and the temporarily lost performance. The estimate of 50 to 200 percent of annual salary is widely quoted. It is a broad expert range. It cannot be used as a universal average, because the role, the market, and the organisation's processes strongly influence it.

It is worth building your own cost model instead. How long does the position stay unfilled? How much management time does selection require? When does a new joiner reach the expected level of independence? Three numbers, and you already have a more accurate picture than any quoted percentage gives you.

## Three questions about fit

**1. Do the requirements of the role and the person's preferences match?**

Personality can help articulate the kind of working environment in which someone functions more easily over time. Requirements have to be established through job analysis, and workload from direct experience. Performance, burnout, or suitability do not follow from a single dimension. A high <DimBadge code="C" label="Conscientiousness" /> score, for instance, does not mean someone will cope with constant replanning.

**2. Are the working rules between manager and team member clear?**

Differing preferences about planning, communication, or decision-making can turn into tension when expectations go unsaid. The profile does not predict conflict here. It supplies topics for a concrete conversation. How detailed a plan do we need, when may we deviate from the process, and how do we flag risks?

**3. Are personal and organisational values aligned?**

According to the 2005 meta-analysis by Kristof-Brown and colleagues, person-environment fit is associated with commitment and intention to stay. Value fit is best examined through the organisation's actual norms, its decisions, and a separate values assessment. A team or a culture cannot be labelled as low in Honesty-Humility. Nor does organisational game-playing follow from one person's score.

## When should you ask these questions?

Asking these three questions after a resignation is too late. An exit interview teaches the organisation something, but it no longer retains that person. A retention conversation works while no decision has been made.

Two moments suggest themselves. One is the end of the probation period, when the person already has experience of the real workload. The other is the threshold of a role or project change. At both it is natural to ask what worked and what did not.

The profile supplies a topic list for that conversation. The reason is the organisation's interest, and the topic is the person's experience.

## What can it be used for in selection?

<KeyInsight>
Do not use trita's development-oriented profile to screen candidates out or to make a standalone suitability decision. Use in selection requires job-specific validation, a fairness review, and multiple data sources.
</KeyInsight>

This is not excessive caution. SIOP's 2018 validation principles prescribe exactly this. The requirements of a role have to be derived from structured job analysis. A personality profile can at most be one further data source alongside structured interviews, work samples, experience, and motivation. That too requires validation for the given use.

## What should you watch for in an existing team?

A sustained change in initiative, participation, or feedback can warrant a supportive conversation. Not every departure is preceded by such a phase. The same change can equally indicate overload, a situation in someone's private life, or a work organisation problem. The profile adds no further information here.

The difference between self-report and others' descriptions shows a different perspective. It does not show change over time. Change can only be discussed on the basis of comparable, repeated measurements, taking into account measurement uncertainty and team composition.

## Limitations and sources

The research cited describes average, group-level associations. A personality profile is not a diagnosis, does not prove an intention to resign, and does not replace a conversation with the person. The Gallup figure concerns the labour market of a single country and cannot be transferred directly to a Hungarian SME context.
