## Not invented at a desk

Most popular personality tests are built by inventing an appealing framework first and writing questions for it afterwards. HEXACO took the opposite route: it grew out of psychology's so-called lexical tradition. The core idea is simple: whatever matters in everyday human differences, language eventually coins a word for. Collect a language's personality-describing words, look at how they cluster in large samples of self-descriptions, and the structure emerges.

This work has been done in many languages, and by the early 2000s the same six factors kept returning in the analyses of more than a dozen of them. One early signal of the sixth — Honesty–Humility — came precisely from a Hungarian vocabulary study. Michael Ashton and Kibeom Lee organised the findings into the model; the name is an acronym: **H**onesty–Humility, **E**motionality, e**X**traversion, **A**greeableness, **C**onscientiousness, **O**penness.

<Callout>
HEXACO does not sort people into types. It shows where you stand on six scales — and both directions are workable: the scales have no “good” or “bad” end, only settings that pay off in different situations.
</Callout>

## The six dimensions

**<DimBadge code="H" label="Honesty–Humility" /> — fairness and modesty.** The territory of sincerity, fairness, greed avoidance, and modesty. Higher scorers avoid manipulation and don't seek the spotlight; lower scorers assert their interests more confidently and are more at home with the instruments of status. At work it surfaces around recognition, “who presents the visible work”, and fair play. Most well-known tests — the classic Big Five and MBTI included — do not measure this as a standalone scale; [here is why the difference matters](/blog/hexaco-vs-mbti-why-it-matters).

**<DimBadge code="E" label="Emotionality" /> — emotional sensitivity.** The scale of proneness to worry, seeking support, and strong emotional attachment. Higher scores signal a more sensitive risk radar and stronger bonds; lower scores signal greater emotional distance and risk tolerance. It is not simply the reverse of the Big Five's “emotional stability” — the emphasis sits elsewhere.

**<DimBadge code="X" label="Extraversion" /> — social energy.** How energising social settings are, and how natural the centre of attention feels. Higher scores signal lively participation and easy relationship-building; lower scores signal deeper individual focus and fewer but more concentrated social moments.

**<DimBadge code="A" label="Agreeableness" /> — patience and cooperation.** The scale of forgiveness, flexibility, and conflict avoidance. Higher scores promise smooth collaboration; lower scores promise faster confrontation and a more critical filter. A team needs both — that is why the “designated critic” practice from [our team-profile article](/blog/reading-your-teams-personality-profile) works.

**<DimBadge code="C" label="Conscientiousness" /> — organisation and discipline.** The territory of planning, perseverance, and thoroughness. Higher scores signal consistent execution; lower scores a more flexible, improvisational working style. What an outlier score can mean in a team — and what it cannot — is what Dávid shows in [our heatmap case](/blog/one-team-one-heatmap).

**<DimBadge code="O" label="Openness" /> — curiosity.** Seeking novel approaches, aesthetic sensitivity, and intellectual curiosity. Higher scores signal idea-richness and appetite for change; lower scores a preference for proven solutions and stability.

## Degrees, not drawers

All six dimensions are continuous scales. The question is never whether someone is extraverted, but how much — and within a team, the size of a difference shows, not just its direction. This structural point is what separates graded models from type-based tests; the detailed comparison is in [our MBTI article](/blog/hexaco-vs-mbti-why-it-matters).

## What does trita measure from this?

trita's questionnaire (TSFI) measures these six dimensions, built on a freely usable item pool established in research. The short form is 60 statements, roughly 9 minutes. Results appear on six 0–100 scales and can be compared with invited colleagues' feedback — because [self-image is only half of the full picture](/blog/why-self-assessment-is-not-enough).

## Three common misunderstandings

**“There is an ideal profile.”** There isn't. Both ends of every scale are workable — the question is always fit: task, role, team.

**“The score is a verdict.”** A score is a self-report snapshot with measurement uncertainty. A few points apart is not a difference — and a large gap is a conversation topic, not a label.

**“Personality equals ability.”** HEXACO does not measure intelligence, knowledge, or performance — it describes a characteristic way of operating. A strong professional is a strong professional with any profile.

## Sources and limitations

The lexical research supports the model's structure — not that any specific questionnaire automatically measures well: that is always the instrument's own burden. The dimension descriptions above follow the official HEXACO scale descriptions; at facet level the picture is more nuanced.
