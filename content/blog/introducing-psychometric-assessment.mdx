The fate of an assessment is decided before it is announced. If participants cannot see the purpose, the path the data takes, and the possible consequences, completing it becomes an obligation and the results are distorted. According to the 2004 meta-analysis by Hausknecht and colleagues, reactions to selection procedures are strongly influenced by the perceived fairness and transparency of the procedure.

## Resistance comes from uncertainty

„What will it be used for?” „Who will see it?” „Could this have consequences for my work?” These are not irrational questions. In a workplace setting, assessment results are sensitive data. And there is a power difference between a manager and a team member to begin with.

<Callout>
Do not start with the test, start with the purpose. First record which team question you are seeking an answer to. Then what the results cannot be used for. The order matters.
</Callout>

## Seven questions to settle before the announcement

- What exactly is the purpose of the assessment?
- Has the instrument been validated for this purpose and in this language context?
- Who sees the individual, the aggregated, and the raw data?
- How long will the results be kept, and how can deletion be requested?
- What feedback does the participant receive?
- Which HR decisions must the results never be used for?
- How can someone say no, or later withdraw their sharing, without disadvantage?

Have an answer to all seven before you announce anything. If one is missing, say honestly that it is still open.

## Who should see the results?

<ResultAccessFigure locale="en" />

Three practical arrangements occur most often. These are not models ranked by research. The right choice depends on the purpose, the team's situation, and the data protection framework.

**Only the participant sees their own result.** The leader and the team get an aggregate view. This is the most protective arrangement, and it still works for individual development. In exchange, the team conversation will be less concrete.

**The participant decides about sharing.** Everyone chooses who they share their profile with. Here you have to watch for normative pressure, because group expectation easily overrides voluntariness.

**The facilitator sees everything, the team sees the aggregate.** The consultant works from the full picture, while only an aggregated view goes to the group. This model works best in sensitive situations. It requires a well-prepared facilitator and confidentiality set out in writing.

## Three principles for the communication

**1. Development, not evaluation.** Say concretely which conversation the assessment supports. Say also that a score is not a diagnosis, not a performance review, and not a verdict on suitability.

**2. Voluntariness and a real choice.** A mandatory or high-stakes situation increases distortion in the answers. Voluntariness is an ethical and trust principle. It does not, however, guarantee undistorted data, because self-selection and missing answers will still occur.

**3. Data handling settled in advance.** Do not promise security in general terms. Name who sees what, where the data is stored, and how long it stays. Describe export, deletion, and feedback too.

## Five steps of the introduction

**1. Define the purpose and the limits.** Write in one sentence which question you are seeking an answer to. Then list what you will not use the results for.

**2. Give the information before the decision.** Before the invitation to participate, there should be a clear description of the process, the data, and the option to decline.

**3. Set an example without creating pressure.** Sharing your own result can be a credible gesture. No expectation towards the others may follow from it.

**4. Leave the right to share intact.** Do not ask for individual profiles to be made public straight away. Not sharing remains a decision to be respected later too.

**5. Interpret the results with a facilitator.** The length of the workshop is determined by team size and purpose. Keep the focus on concrete cooperation situations and on working rules that can be tried out.

<KeyInsight>
An assessment gives a shared vocabulary when the results produce verifiable questions and concrete agreements about working together. Nothing comes of labels.
</KeyInsight>

## A template for the announcement

> I would like to start a voluntary assessment in the team. Our aim is to sharpen our shared working rules, for example how we prepare decisions and how we flag deadlines.
>
> It takes about ten minutes to complete. Everyone sees their own result. Only an aggregated view goes to the team, with no individual scores. I will not see anyone's individual result either.
>
> We will not use the results for selection, pay, or individual performance reviews. Declining to take part has no consequences whatsoever, and you do not owe me a reason. The data will be kept for [X] months, and deletion can be requested at any time.
>
> We will go through the results together in a facilitated conversation on [date]. If you would like to ask anything beforehand, please do.

Rewrite this template for your own situation. Fill the bracketed parts with concrete details.

## What about during a conflict?

In an acute conflict, publicly sharing individual profiles is especially risky. Scores easily turn into personal accusations. This does not mean all measurement is forbidden at such times. With a clear purpose, explicit consent, aggregated data, and a well-prepared facilitator, the process can support the work of surfacing what is going on.

It is not only the Honesty-Humility and Agreeableness dimensions that are sensitive. Any personal result can be used for labelling if a shared interpretive and data-handling frame is missing.
