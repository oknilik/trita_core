## Where the resistance comes from

The resistance is usually not against the assessment itself. It is against what people believe the assessment will lead to.

"They'll use this to decide whether I'm good enough." "This data will be used against me." "This means they don't trust us."

<Callout>
These fears work without any rational basis. If the rollout communication doesn't address them up front, resistance is guaranteed – even when your intention is entirely different.
</Callout>

## The one decision that determines success: who gets the data?

The most important decision in the rollout is not the tool – it's data access. There are three models:

For most teams, the first model is the safest way to start. As trust grows, sharing expands voluntarily.

## Three principles of communication

**1. Development, not evaluation**

"This isn't about labelling anyone. It's about everyone getting to know themselves better – and optionally sharing with the team if they want to."

**2. Voluntary participation**

If participation is mandatory, the data becomes distorted – people answer what's expected of them. Voluntary participation produces better data and more trust.

**3. The result belongs to them**

The profile belongs to the individual. The organisation doesn't get it automatically. This is not a weakness of the rollout – it is the reason people genuinely take part.

## The rollout, step by step

**Step 1 – Give context:** In a short team meeting, explain the goal. Not "getting to know each other", but concretely: "I want us to see more clearly where our natural strengths are and where it's worth asking for help."

**Step 2 – The leader goes first:** If you complete it first and share your own results with the team, that signal beats any communication. It shows this is not a weapon – it's a mirror.

**Step 3 – Sharing is each person's decision:** Give it time and space. Don't ask people to share their results right away. Those who feel confident will share.

**Step 4 – Interpret together:** Once enough team members have shared, hold a one-hour workshop – not with analysis, but with questions. "Where did you recognise each other? What surprised you?"

<KeyInsight>
The joint interpretation workshop is not about the numbers. It's about the team gaining a shared language for handling differences – without anyone experiencing each other's style as personal criticism.
</KeyInsight>

## When should you NOT introduce psychometric assessment?

When the team has an active conflict or a trust crisis. In that situation the profile becomes a weapon – in both directions. Stabilise the relationships first; the tool comes after.

The <DimBadge code="H" label="Honesty-Humility" /> and <DimBadge code="A" label="Agreeableness" /> dimensions are especially sensitive here: low scores in a conflicted team easily turn into personal accusations when the interpretive frame is missing.
