## The rollout is decided before the announcement

The moment you announce an assessment, three questions form in the team: “What will it be used for?” “Who will see it?” “Can this affect my job?” These are legitimate questions - at work, results are sensitive data, and there is an inherent power difference between manager and team member. The good news: if you have ready answers to all three at the moment of the announcement, trust and willingness to participate start from a completely different place.

<Callout>
Don't start with the test - start with the purpose: what team question are you trying to answer, and what will you not do with the results.
</Callout>

## A checklist before you announce

Settle these seven points before telling anyone:

- What exactly is the assessment for?
- Has the instrument been validated for this purpose and this linguistic-cultural context?
- Who sees individual, aggregated, and raw data?
- How long are results kept, and how can deletion be requested?
- What feedback does each participant receive?
- Which HR decisions are the results forbidden to inform?
- How can someone decline, or later withdraw sharing, without disadvantage?

This access model protects individual data while allowing the team to interpret how it works together. Before announcing the assessment, state clearly who will see what and what the results may be used for.

## What the announcement can sound like

A script you can adapt to your own situation:

> “We want to be more deliberate about how we work together, and we'll use a self-assessment questionnaire for that. Completing it is voluntary. Each person's individual result is visible only to them; in the workshop we'll work with the aggregated, team-level picture. The results will not be used for performance reviews, pay, or any employment decision. Anyone who prefers not to take part will face no disadvantage whatsoever.”

These few sentences state the three principles: **development, not judgement** (what it is for and what it is not), **genuine voluntariness** (no is a fine answer), **concrete data governance** (who sees what - not a vague promise of “security”).

## Five steps of a good rollout

**1. Fix the purpose and the prohibitions.** In one sentence: what question are we answering, and what will the results not be used for.

**2. Inform before the decision.** Before inviting participation, provide a clear description of the process, the data, and the option to decline.

**3. Lead by example without creating a norm.** Sharing your own result can be a credible gesture - as long as it never becomes an unspoken expectation for others.

**4. Preserve the right not to share.** Don't ask for individual profiles to be made public right away. Not sharing remains a decision to respect later, too.

**5. Interpret with facilitation.** The workshop's focus should not be “decoding” people, but concrete collaboration situations and working rules to try out.

<KeyInsight>
An assessment provides a shared language when the results produce verifiable questions and concrete collaboration agreements - not labels.
</KeyInsight>

## What about conflict situations?

In an acute conflict, publicly sharing individual profiles is risky: scores easily turn into personal accusations. That does not rule out measurement - with a clear purpose, explicit consent, aggregated data, and a trained facilitator, the process can help map the situation. The key is the frame: without shared interpretation rules, any personal result can become a label.

## Sources and limitations

Voluntariness is an ethical and trust principle, but it does not guarantee unbiased data: self-selection and missing responses remain possible. A leader's example must not turn into an expectation. A score is not a diagnosis, a performance review, or a suitability verdict - which is why the prohibited uses are worth fixing in writing, in advance.
