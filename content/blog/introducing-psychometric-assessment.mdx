## Uncertainty is a major source of resistance

“What will this be used for?” “Who will see it?” “Could it affect my job?” These are not irrational questions. Workplace assessment involves sensitive information and an existing power difference between employee and employer.

<Callout>
A common source of resistance is uncertainty about the purpose, the path of the data, and the possible consequences.
</Callout>

Communication should therefore not begin with “we are taking a test”. First define the team question, what the result must not be used for, and which decisions will not be based on it.

## The questions to settle before rollout

Data access matters, but it is not the only decision. At minimum, clarify:

- What is the exact purpose of the assessment?
- Has the instrument been validated for that purpose and language–culture context?
- Who can see individual, aggregate, and raw data?
- How long is the result retained, and how can deletion be requested?
- What feedback will participants receive?
- Which HR decisions are explicitly out of scope?
- How can someone decline without disadvantage or later withdraw sharing?

These are practical options, not three exhaustive models ranked by research. The right arrangement depends on purpose, team context, and data governance.

## Three communication principles

**1. Development, not evaluation.** Explain the conversation the assessment is meant to support. A score is not a diagnosis, performance rating, or suitability verdict.

**2. Voluntary participation and genuine choice.** Mandatory or high-stakes settings can increase response distortion. Voluntariness is an important ethical and trust principle, but it does not guarantee unbiased data; self-selection and missing responses can remain.

**3. Data governance explained in advance.** Do not promise “safety” in general terms. State who sees what, where the data is stored, how long it is retained, and how exports, deletion, and feedback are handled.

## A practical rollout

**1. Define the purpose and limits.** For example: “We want to improve our working agreements. Results will not be used for selection, pay, or individual performance evaluation.”

**2. Inform before asking for a decision.** Participants need an understandable account of the process, data, and option to decline before the invitation.

**3. Leaders may model openness without creating a norm.** Sharing their own result can be a credible gesture, but it must not create an expectation that others will do the same.

**4. Preserve the right not to share.** Do not ask for immediate disclosure of individual profiles. A decision not to share must continue to be respected.

**5. Use facilitated interpretation.** Workshop length depends on team size, purpose, and context. Focus on concrete situations and testable working agreements, not decoding people.

<KeyInsight>
An assessment can create a shared language when results become testable questions and practical agreements – not labels.
</KeyInsight>

## What about active conflict?

Public sharing of individual profiles is especially risky during active conflict: scores can quickly become accusations. This does not make every form of assessment inappropriate. With a clear purpose, explicit agreement, aggregate evidence, and a trained facilitator, a diagnostic process may help explore the situation.

The risk is not limited to H or A. Any personal result can be weaponised when the interpretive and governance framework is missing.
