trita's assessment describes how people operate along six dimensions. Those six dimensions are not our invention: they come from one of personality psychology's most thoroughly researched frameworks, the six-factor personality model, known in the scientific literature as HEXACO. In this article we show where the model comes from, what the six scales measure - and how we built trita's own questionnaire on top of it.

## Not invented at a desk

Most popular personality tests are built by inventing an appealing framework first and writing questions for it afterwards. The six-factor model took the opposite route: it grew out of psychology's so-called lexical tradition. The core idea is simple: whatever matters in everyday human differences, language eventually coins a word for. Collect a language's personality-describing words, look at how they cluster in large samples of self-descriptions, and the structure emerges.

This work has been done in many languages, and by the early 2000s the same six factors kept returning in the analyses of more than a dozen of them. One early signal of the sixth - Honesty-Humility - came precisely from a Hungarian vocabulary study. Michael Ashton and Kibeom Lee organised the findings into the model under the name **HEXACO**; it is an acronym: **H**onesty-Humility, **E**motionality, e**X**traversion, **A**greeableness, **C**onscientiousness, **O**penness. This model is the foundation of trita's measurement.

<Callout>
The six-factor model does not sort people into types: six scales show what is characteristic of you - and neither direction is “good” or “bad”; each shows its advantages in different situations.
</Callout>

## The six dimensions

**<DimBadge code="H" label="Honesty-Humility" /> - fairness and modesty.** The territory of sincerity, fairness, greed avoidance, and modesty. Higher scorers avoid manipulation and don't seek the spotlight; lower scorers assert their interests more confidently and are less uncomfortable with competition for status. At work it surfaces around recognition, fair treatment, and who presents the visible work. Most well-known tests - the classic Big Five and MBTI included - do not measure this as a standalone scale; [here is why the difference matters](/blog/hexaco-vs-mbti-why-it-matters).

**<DimBadge code="E" label="Emotionality" /> - emotional sensitivity.** The scale of proneness to worry, seeking support, and strong emotional attachment. Higher scores signal a more sensitive risk radar and stronger bonds; lower scores signal greater emotional distance and risk tolerance. It is not simply the reverse of the Big Five's “emotional stability” - the emphasis sits elsewhere.

**<DimBadge code="X" label="Extraversion" /> - social energy.** How energising social settings are, and how natural the centre of attention feels. Higher scores signal lively participation and easy relationship-building; lower scores signal deeper individual focus and fewer but more concentrated social moments.

**<DimBadge code="A" label="Agreeableness" /> - patience and cooperation.** The scale of forgiveness, flexibility, and conflict avoidance. Higher scores signal a more patient, conciliatory approach; lower scorers confront sooner and look at things with a more critical eye. A team needs both - that is why the “designated critic” practice from [our team-profile article](/blog/reading-your-teams-personality-profile) works.

**<DimBadge code="C" label="Conscientiousness" /> - organisation and discipline.** The territory of planning, perseverance, and thoroughness. Higher scores signal consistent execution; lower scores a more flexible, improvisational working style. Our [anonymised team story](/blog/one-team-one-heatmap) shows how an aggregate report can start a conversation about planning without revealing anyone's individual result.

**<DimBadge code="O" label="Openness" /> - curiosity.** This is the home of seeking novel approaches, aesthetic sensitivity, and intellectual curiosity. Higher scores signal idea-richness and appetite for change; lower scores that someone prefers to build on proven solutions and stability.

## Degrees, not drawers

All six dimensions are continuous scales. The question is never whether someone is extraverted, but how much - and within a team, the size of a difference shows, not just its direction. This structural point is what separates graded models from type-based tests; the detailed comparison is in [our MBTI article](/blog/hexaco-vs-mbti-why-it-matters).

## How does trita build on this?

trita did not adopt a ready-made test; it built its own questionnaire on the model: the TSFI. The items' foundation came from the IPIP - a freely usable item pool established in research, whose items were developed for the six-factor model's scales. From that pool we selected our own item set, produced the Hungarian adaptation, and keep refining the questionnaire and its scoring through our own research.

The short form is 60 statements, roughly 10 minutes to complete. Results appear on six 0-100 scales and can be compared with invited colleagues' feedback - because [self-image is only half of the full picture](/blog/why-self-assessment-is-not-enough).

## Three common misunderstandings

**“There is an ideal profile.”** There isn't. Both directions of every scale are workable - the question is always how well the profile fits the task, the role, and the team.

**“The score is a verdict.”** A score is a self-report snapshot with measurement uncertainty. A few points apart is not a difference - and a large gap is a conversation topic, not a label.

**“Personality equals ability.”** The six-factor model does not measure intelligence, knowledge, or performance - it describes a characteristic way of operating. A strong professional is a strong professional with any profile.

## Sources and limitations

The lexical research supports the model's structure - it does not guarantee that any specific questionnaire measures well: that always depends on the instrument itself. The dimension descriptions above follow the official HEXACO scale descriptions; at facet level the picture is more nuanced.
