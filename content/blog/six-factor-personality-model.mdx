One of the most thoroughly researched frameworks in personality psychology describes how someone works along six dimensions. The literature knows it as HEXACO, Michael Ashton and Kibeom Lee systematised it, and trita's core measurement is built on it. The sixth dimension, Honesty-Humility, is the one missing from most well-known questionnaires.

## Our words shaped it

Most popular personality tests are built by someone inventing a framework and then writing questions for it. The six-factor model came about the other way round. It grew out of the lexical tradition in personality psychology. That tradition starts from the idea that language eventually coins a word for any human quality that matters in everyday life.

The method itself is simple. You collect the personality-describing words of a language, ask a large sample of people to describe themselves with them, then see which words travel together. The clustering reveals the underlying structure. This work has been done in many languages.

According to Ashton and Lee's 2007 review, the same six factors emerged in deep vocabulary analyses of more than a dozen languages. The sixth, Honesty-Humility, was also signalled by an early Hungarian vocabulary study. The name is an acronym: **H**onesty-Humility, **E**motionality, e**X**traversion, **A**greeableness, **C**onscientiousness, **O**penness.

<Callout>
The six-factor model does not sort people into rigid categories. It shows on six scales what is characteristic of you. Neither direction is better than the other, and each shows its advantages in different situations.
</Callout>

## The six dimensions

**<DimBadge code="H" label="Honesty-Humility" /> — fairness and modesty.** The territory of sincerity, fair treatment, avoiding greed, and modesty. Someone scoring higher avoids manipulation and does not seek the spotlight. Someone scoring lower asserts their interests more confidently. At work it surfaces wherever the question is who presents the result to the client. Most well-known questionnaires do not measure this as a separate scale, and [that is a real difference](/blog/hexaco-vs-mbti-why-it-matters).

**<DimBadge code="E" label="Emotionality" /> — emotional sensitivity.** The scale of proneness to worry, seeking support, and strong attachment. A higher score indicates greater sensitivity to danger, a lower one more emotional distance and risk tolerance. Reading it as a simple inverse of the Big Five emotional stability scale is misleading, because the emphases fall elsewhere. In practice you see it in who first flags the risks in a project.

**<DimBadge code="X" label="Extraversion" /> — social energy.** How much social settings energise someone, how natural it feels to be the centre of attention. A higher score indicates lively participation and easy relationship-building. A lower one indicates deeper individual focus and a more reserved presence. In a meeting of ten people, this is the fastest dimension to observe.

**<DimBadge code="A" label="Agreeableness" /> — patience and cooperation.** The territory of forgiveness, flexibility, and conflict avoidance. Someone scoring higher is more patient and more willing to make peace. Someone scoring lower takes on confrontation sooner and looks at proposals with a more critical eye. A planning debate needs both, which is why the „designated critic” practice works, as we wrote in the [team profile article](/blog/reading-your-teams-personality-profile).

**<DimBadge code="C" label="Conscientiousness" /> — organisation and discipline.** The scale of planning, persistence, and thoroughness. A higher score indicates consistent execution, a lower one a more flexible, improvisational way of working. This dimension tends to come up when a team argues about how detailed its interim deadlines should be. Our [anonymised team story](/blog/one-team-one-heatmap) is about exactly that.

**<DimBadge code="O" label="Openness" /> — curiosity.** Seeking out new approaches, aesthetic receptiveness, and intellectual interest. A higher score indicates a wealth of ideas and an appetite for change. A lower one builds on proven solutions and stability. When a process is being redesigned, this is typically the dimension that divides a team.

## Degrees, not boxes

All six dimensions measure on a continuous scale. So the question is never whether someone is extraverted. The question is how much. And in a team, the size of the difference between members is visible too, not only its direction.

This structural difference is what separates continuous scales from questionnaires that sort people into categories. You will find the detailed comparison in our [MBTI article](/blog/hexaco-vs-mbti-why-it-matters). In trita we also use nuanced pattern descriptions for easier reading. The score remains primary, and the description only helps interpret it.

## How does trita build on this?

We did not adopt an off-the-shelf test. We built our own item bank on the model, and we validate it continuously. Its name is TSFI.

The items are based on IPIP. This is a freely usable item bank used in research, whose statements were developed for the scales of the six-factor model. From that bank we assembled our own set, produced the Hungarian adaptation, and we refine the items and their weighting on our own data.

The short version consists of 60 statements and takes about ten minutes. The result appears on six 0-100 scales. It can also be compared with feedback from invited colleagues and acquaintances, because [self-image is only half of the full picture](/blog/why-self-assessment-is-not-enough).

## Three common misunderstandings

**„There is an ideal profile.”** There is not. Both directions work on every scale. The question is always how well the profile fits the environment, the task, and the team.

**„The score is a verdict.”** The score is a snapshot based on self-report, with measurement uncertainty. A difference of a few points does not in itself mean a meaningful difference. A larger difference can be a topic for conversation.

**„Personality equals ability.”** The six-factor model does not measure intelligence, knowledge, or performance. It measures characteristic ways of working. A strong professional stays a strong professional with any personality pattern.

## Limitations and sources

The lexical research supports the structure of the model. It does not guarantee that a particular item bank measures well. That always depends on the given instrument, ours included. Our dimension descriptions follow the official HEXACO scale descriptions, and at the level of the facets the picture is more nuanced still.
