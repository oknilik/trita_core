An important client delivery was approaching while Anna’s team kept getting stuck at the same points in their work. She could sense the tension but could not yet see exactly what was sustaining it. Through an anonymised team’s experience, this article shows how a heatmap can become the starting point for a shared conversation rather than a ready-made explanation.

<Callout>
The story is based on a real team workshop. We anonymised the names, organisational details, and scores, and condensed the dialogue rather than presenting it as verbatim quotation. This protects the participants, but it does not mean that the same score pattern has the same cause in every team.
</Callout>

## How the team worked before the delivery

The story follows a client-delivery team at a software company. Anna is the project manager and also works directly with the client. Bence is the senior developer, Dávid the other developer, Csilla the designer, Eszter owns testing and the day-to-day client contact, and Feri is the account manager who brings in new business.

The team performs well, but meetings run long and the same two people usually do the talking. Dávid often questions individual process steps. Open conflict is rare, yet the real difficulties still do not surface in retrospectives.

So Anna started an assessment-supported team workshop, following the [rollout principles](/blog/introducing-psychometric-assessment): participation was voluntary and the purpose was agreed in advance. Each participant separately consented to showing their results by name, and everyone understood that they could later withdraw their results from the shared view. ## A heatmap is not an explanation

Anna first noticed the differences within the team. On Extraversion, Bence’s score of 26 sat far from Anna’s 81. On Conscientiousness, Dávid’s 38 differed from the others’ scores between 69 and 84. The widest range appeared on Honesty–Humility, between Feri’s 31 and Eszter’s 83.

These values did not explain why meetings ran long, why Dávid challenged process steps, or how recognition was distributed across the team. They gave the team a reason to examine specific situations and ask whether the assessment patterns connected with their own experience.

## The leader starts with herself

Anna did not assume that Bence stayed quiet because of his Extraversion score. Instead, she recalled what happened in meetings: “Feri and I often speak early and hold the floor for a long time. How do the rest of you see it?” It is the kind of leadership gesture described in [our article on psychological safety](/blog/can-psychological-safety-be-measured): the leader makes her own observation open to examination instead of using a profile to explain a quieter team member.

The team tried collecting everyone’s written input before a decision and starting the meeting with a summary of those views. This also gave Bence more room to develop his arguments. The team introduced it as an experiment to review after a few weeks, not as a permanent rule.

## An outlier score is not a list of faults

It would have been easy to turn Dávid’s Conscientiousness score of 38 into an “undisciplined” label. Instead, the team examined the specific situations in which his way of working created difficulty and those in which it brought a useful perspective. The conversation showed that Dávid often questioned the purpose of process steps. In the past, he had identified two regular reports that nobody used.

The profile did not decide how Dávid’s role should change. Based on their shared work experience and the conversation, they agreed that he would schedule his own interim milestones and give a clear status update at the end of each sprint. He would also collect proposals for simplifying processes. They treated this as a working agreement to test, not a role assigned on the basis of his personality.

## A sensitive dimension needs a concrete situation

Honesty–Humility is particularly easy to misread as a moral judgement. In the workshop, nobody inferred a person’s intentions or integrity from their score. Instead, the team looked for an observable situation in which members experienced the balance between recognition and contribution differently.

In this situation, Feri presented the results to the client while Eszter’s testing before the demonstration was barely visible outside the team. This did not mean that either person had acted in bad faith. The question was how to make contributions more visible without assigning motives or roles to people on the basis of their scores.

The team tried regularly asking about less visible work during retrospectives and naming contributors in the client presentation. They planned to keep or revise the practice later, depending on whether it helped the team experience recognition as fairer.

<KeyInsight>
The heatmap did not provide a ready-made explanation of how the team worked. It helped them begin with observable situations, discuss their assumptions, and turn those discussions into working agreements they could test.
</KeyInsight>

## What can the team learn from the trials?

A month later, the team reviewed its experience together. The written round helped bring more perspectives into view before meetings. At Dávid’s suggestion, they retired an unused report. The conversation about less visible work felt awkward at first, but it surfaced contributions Anna had not previously known about.

They dropped another idea, meeting-free Wednesday, after two weeks because it did not help their shared work. That was not a failure of the workshop, but part of experimenting. These experiences do not prove that the heatmap caused the changes. They show how a team can test working agreements after an assessment and then keep, revise, or discard them in light of experience.

## Limitations and related reading

This anonymised practical example is not research evidence. We changed identifying details, and there is no reason to expect the same steps to produce the same outcomes in another team. Conversation and experimentation prompted by assessment patterns can offer a useful framework, but their value also depends on participants’ consent, the quality of facilitation, the organisational context, and follow-up.
