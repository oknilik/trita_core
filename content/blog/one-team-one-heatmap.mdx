Six people, one delivery deadline — and a leader who can feel that something keeps getting stuck, without being able to say exactly where. This article walks through such a team's path from heatmap to working rules.

<Callout>
The team is fictional: the names, the scores, and the dialogue too. The patterns, however, were chosen precisely because they come up in small teams again and again. If you recognise your own team, that is no coincidence.
</Callout>

## The team: six people, one deadline

Take a thirty-person software company and one of its six-person delivery teams. Anna leads it: she is the project manager and also faces the client. Bence is the senior developer, Dávid the other developer, Csilla the designer, Eszter owns testing and the day-to-day client contact, and Feri is the account manager who brings in the extensions.

The team performs well, but three things keep grinding. Meetings run long, and the same two people always do the talking. Dávid and the processes have a tense relationship. And although open conflict is rare, somehow nothing substantial ever gets said in the retros.

So Anna started an assessment-supported team workshop — following the [rollout rules](/blog/introducing-psychometric-assessment): voluntary, with a purpose fixed in advance. In the kickoff conversation the team itself chose to look at the results by name in the workshop. After everyone completed the questionnaire, this is what they saw:

## At first glance: three patterns

As described in [our article on reading a team profile](/blog/reading-your-teams-personality-profile), three glances: average, spread, edges. Anna picked out three things:

- **Extraversion: wide spread.** Anna 81, Feri 72 — Bence 26. Suddenly the meeting dynamic is not a character question but a distribution.
- **Conscientiousness: five high, one outlier.** The team sits between 69 and 84 — Dávid at 38.
- **Honesty–Humility: the widest band.** Everything between Eszter's 83 and Feri's 31.

None of these is a diagnosis. Each is a question worth asking.

## The first question belonged to the leader

Anna's first reaction was honest: “Our meetings look the way they do because Feri and I carry the conversation — and now I can see it.” In the workshop, nobody asked Bence why he stays quiet; Anna named her own pattern. It is the kind of leadership gesture [our psychological-safety article](/blog/can-psychological-safety-be-measured) describes: an “I'm not sure about this — what do you see?” opens more than a ready answer.

The rule they agreed on is simple: before a decision, everyone gives written input, and the meeting starts with a summary of the written round. Two weeks later, Bence argued more sharply in writing than he ever had out loud.

## An outlier score is not a list of faults

Next to Dávid's Conscientiousness score of 38 sat another number: Openness 79, one of the team's highest. The conversation confirmed what the heatmap only hinted at: Dávid is not “undisciplined” — he is the one who asks, at every process step, what it is for. And twice he had been right: two reporting steps genuinely went unread.

The agreement went both ways. Dávid now schedules his own interim milestones — in exchange, the end-of-sprint status is sacred for him too. And he became the owner of process-simplification proposals: the critique turned into a role.

## The most delicate topic: invisible work

The team approached the Honesty–Humility spread carefully — this is the dimension that slips most easily into moral judgement, which it is not: a lower score is not unethical conduct but a different setting of self-assertion and modesty.

The concrete situation looked like this. Feri finds it natural that he presents the results to the client. Eszter finds it natural that nobody sees the night-before-demo test round. Neither is doing anything wrong — they simply think differently about visible and invisible work.

The rule became explicit here too: every two weeks, five minutes in the retro — “what did you carry that doesn't show?” — and the client presentation names who did what.

<KeyInsight>
The heatmap answered none of the questions — it asked all three. The team gained more from three good questions and two testable rules than from twenty minutes of staring at scores.
</KeyInsight>

## One month later

Meetings are shorter, because the written round absorbs the warm-up. Dávid officially retired one reporting step. The “invisible work” round has run twice — once it started from a distinctly awkward silence, and both times something surfaced that Anna hadn't known about.

Nothing became perfect: the third proposed rule (meeting-free Wednesday) quietly died after two weeks. That is healthy. The workshop is not an oath-taking but a series of experiments: a month later they reviewed — two rules stuck, one didn't — and moved on.

## Sources and limitations

This story is an illustration, not case-study evidence: the team is fictional, the scores hand-picked, and in reality this much rarely settles in a single month. The pattern → question → testable rule sequence, however, works exactly like this in real engagements. The underlying research and the limits are covered in the related articles:

*The scores in this article are 0–100 display-scale demo values — not percentiles, and not real people's data. In a real team, sharing individual results is always the members' decision; in this story the team chose the by-name view together at the start of the process.*
