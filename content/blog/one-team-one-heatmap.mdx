An important client delivery was approaching while Anna’s team kept getting stuck at the same points in their work. She could sense the tension but could not yet see exactly what was sustaining it. Through an anonymised team’s experience, this article shows how an aggregate team report can become the starting point for a shared conversation rather than a ready-made explanation.

<Callout>
The story is based on a real team workshop. We anonymised the names, organisational details, and aggregate values, and condensed the dialogue rather than presenting it as verbatim quotation. No other member’s individual result was visible to the leader or team in the original process either.
</Callout>

## How the team worked before the delivery

The story follows a client-delivery team at a software company. Anna is the project manager and also works directly with the client. Bence is the senior developer, Dávid the other developer, Csilla the designer, Eszter owns testing and the day-to-day client contact, and Feri is the account manager who brings in new business.

The team performs well, but meetings run long and the same two people usually do the talking. Dávid often questions individual process steps. Open conflict is rare, yet the real difficulties still do not surface in retrospectives.

So Anna started an assessment-supported team workshop, following the [rollout principles](/blog/introducing-psychometric-assessment): participation was voluntary and the purpose was agreed in advance. After completing the questionnaire, each person saw only their own profile. The consultant used access-protected individual data to prepare an aggregate team picture and shared interpretation. ## The report does not show where each person stands

The report summarised the team’s collective profile and its internal diversity across dimensions. The shared interpretation suggested meaningful differences in how people participated socially. The team was not uniform in its relationship with planning and structured processes either, while recognition and less visible contributions emerged as a topic worth examining together.

The report named nobody and did not explain why meetings ran long or how recognition was distributed. It provided a frame for examining concrete situations and checking whether the shared interpretation connected with the team’s own experience.

## The leader starts with herself

Anna could compare her own profile with the aggregate team picture, but she could not see anyone else’s individual result. She therefore brought her own experience into the conversation: “Feri and I often speak early and hold the floor for a long time. How do the rest of you see it?” It is the kind of leadership approach described in [our article on psychological safety](/blog/can-psychological-safety-be-measured): Anna questions her own behaviour rather than interpreting another team member’s profile.

The team tried collecting everyone’s written input before a decision and starting the meeting with a summary of those views. This also gave Bence more room to develop his arguments. The team introduced it as an experiment to review after a few weeks, not as a permanent rule.

## Shared interpretation is not a personal label

The report framed different relationships with planning and structured processes as a team-level topic. The workshop did not try to identify who might sit at the edge of the pattern. Instead, the team examined which processes supported the work and which had become ends in themselves. Dávid chose to share that he often questioned the purpose of individual process steps. In the past, he had identified a regular report that nobody used.

The report did not decide how Dávid’s role should change. Based on their shared work experience and the conversation, they agreed that he would schedule his own interim milestones and give a clear status update at the end of each sprint. He would also collect proposals for simplifying processes. They tried this way of working for a few weeks rather than assigning him a new role on the basis of a personality profile.

## A sensitive dimension needs a concrete situation

The report’s statement about fairness could easily have been turned into a moral judgement about individuals. The workshop therefore did not speculate about anyone’s score. Instead, the team looked for an observable situation in which members experienced the balance between recognition and contribution differently.

In this situation, Feri presented the results to the client while Eszter’s testing before the demonstration was barely visible outside the team. This did not mean that either person had acted in bad faith. The question was how to make contributions more visible without assigning motives or roles to one another.

The team tried regularly asking about less visible work during retrospectives and naming contributors in the client presentation. They planned to keep or revise the practice later, depending on whether it helped the team experience recognition as fairer.

<KeyInsight>
The aggregate report did not provide a ready-made explanation of how the team worked, and it revealed nobody’s individual result. It helped them begin with observable situations, discuss their assumptions, and try concrete changes together.
</KeyInsight>

## What can the team learn from the trials?

A month later, the team reviewed its experience together. The written round helped bring more perspectives into view before meetings. At Dávid’s suggestion, they retired an unused report. The conversation about less visible work felt awkward at first, but it surfaced contributions Anna had not previously known about.

They dropped another idea, meeting-free Wednesday, after two weeks because it did not help their shared work. That was not a failure of the workshop, but part of experimenting. These experiences do not prove that the report caused the changes. They show how a team can try changes after an assessment and then keep, revise, or discard them in light of experience.

## Limitations and related reading

This anonymised practical example is not research evidence. We changed identifying details, and there is no reason to expect the same steps to produce the same outcomes in another team. Conversation and experimentation prompted by assessment patterns can offer a useful framework, but their value also depends on participants’ consent, the quality of facilitation, the organisational context, and follow-up.
