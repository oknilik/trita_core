It was Thursday morning, six people sat in the meeting room, and the projector was on. Anna had asked for the assessment three weeks earlier. Now they were seeing the result together for the first time. Two weeks remained until the client project handover.

The mood was cautious. Bence was looking at his laptop, Dávid was leaning back, Eszter was stirring her coffee. I sat at the end of the table as the facilitator, and what I saw was that nobody knew what was coming. That uncertainty is a constant companion of the first half hour after an assessment.

<Callout>
This story is based on a real team workshop. Names, organisational details, and aggregate values have been anonymised, and I render the dialogue in condensed form rather than as verbatim quotes. In the original process, no team member's individual result was visible to the leader or to the team either.
</Callout>

The team worked on a software company's client project. Anna ran the project and the client negotiations. Bence and Dávid developed, Csilla designed, Eszter tested and kept up the daily client contact. Feri brought in new engagements. They performed well, but meetings dragged on, and it was usually the same two people speaking.

## What Anna saw on the screen

On the screen was the aggregate personality picture from the team report. It shows the team average and the diversity within the team along six dimensions. It contains no individual results. After completing the questionnaire, each person saw only their own profile, and only aggregated data entered the shared view.

<TeamReportFigure locale="en" />

Anna's first question was what the numbers mean for the daily work. The report does not answer that question. The screen showed two wide bands, on the <DimBadge code="X" label="Extraversion" /> and <DimBadge code="H" label="Honesty-Humility" /> dimensions. One is about how much someone seeks social participation. The other is about their relationship to fairness and modesty.

The aggregate picture showed where it was worth asking questions. It did no more than that. It named nobody, and it did not explain why meetings dragged on. The team had to bring up the concrete situations.

## Questions instead of explanations

That difference organised the whole day. A measurement can be used in two ways. One use looks for an explanation in it. The other derives questions from it and expects the answers to come from the team's experience.

The explanatory use is faster and more comfortable. If we read the wide Extraversion band as „Dávid must be low, that is why he is so quiet”, the story is complete. That story, however, contains not a single observation. It hands out a label, and nothing will come of a label in the weeks ahead.

The questioning use is slower. Anna started with her own experience. „Feri and I often speak up early and hold the floor for a long time. How do you see it?” She asked about her own way of working, not about someone else's profile.

The answer did not come at once. Csilla was the first to say that she loses the thread in long debates. Bence added that he rarely gets to finish making his points. That was already an observation, not an interpretation.

The team then tried three things for a few weeks:

- Before a decision, everyone gives their view in writing, and the meeting starts with a summary of those.
- Dávid sets his own interim deadlines and reports on progress at the end of the sprint.
- Contributors are named in the client presentation.

None of these was a rule. Each was an experiment. That difference mattered later.

We handled the Honesty-Humility dimension more carefully. It is the easiest one to turn into a moral judgement. We did not guess anyone's score. We looked for a situation in which team members had experienced the distribution of recognition differently.

Feri presented the results to the client. Eszter's pre-demo testing was barely visible outside the team. It did not follow that anyone had acted in bad faith. The aggregate picture showed where it was worth asking questions, and the question was about visibility.

<KeyInsight>
A measurement is worth something when it starts a conversation about observable situations, and the team then tries out concrete changes. A ready-made explanation is more comfortable, but nothing will come of it in the weeks ahead.
</KeyInsight>

## A month later, in the same room

Four weeks later the same people sat in the same meeting room. The handover had happened. This time the projector was dark, and the three experiments were on paper.

The written round stayed. According to Anna, more perspectives became visible before meetings. On Dávid's initiative they retired a recurring report that nobody was using. The conversation about less visible work was awkward at first. Later, though, it surfaced contributions Anna had not known about.

A fourth idea, the meeting-free Wednesday, was dropped after two weeks. It did not help the shared work. That is part of experimenting, not a failure of the workshop. This is where the team learned that a trial can also be discarded.

That second morning was the real measuring point of the story. The first time, nobody knew what was coming. The second time, they were talking about their own experience and barely referring to the measurement. The aggregate picture showed where it was worth asking questions, and that was enough from it.

## Limitations and related reading

This anonymised case is not research evidence. Identifying details have been changed. I have no reason to assume that the same steps would produce the same result in another team. These experiences do not prove that the measurement caused the changes.

A conversation that starts from patterns can be a useful frame. Its value also depends on the participants' consent, the quality of the conversation, the organisational context, and the follow-up. Our three related articles cover this in more detail.
