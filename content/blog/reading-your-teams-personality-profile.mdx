## The individual profile is only the first step

You can read a lot from one person's personality profile. But to understand team dynamics, the individual scores are not what matters most – the distribution and the pattern are.

<Callout>
A team is not the sum of individual profiles. It is the interactions, where individual differences can amplify or cancel each other out.
</Callout>

## What does the heatmap show?

The trita team heatmap shows a grid: rows = team members, columns = dimensions, cells = scores on a 0–100 scale. The goal is not to declare who is "better" – it is to see where the natural strengths are and where the potential friction points lie.

**A homogeneous heatmap** – when everyone shows similar scores along one dimension, that means stability – but also a blind spot. A team where everyone scores high on <DimBadge code="O" label="Openness" /> will be creative, but will struggle to follow procedures.

**A heterogeneous heatmap** – a wide spread along a dimension can be a source of tension – but also of complementarity. A high and a low <DimBadge code="C" label="Conscientiousness" /> person can be a source of conflict if they don't know each other's style – or they can complete each other.

## The five most common team patterns

**1. The high-C team** – everyone is conscientious and organised. Strong results orientation. Risk: rigid, slow to adapt; underperforms on creative tasks.

**2. The high-A team** – high <DimBadge code="A" label="Agreeableness" /> across the board. Harmony-oriented, little open conflict. Risk: clashes never surface – they just sink.

**3. The high-X team** – extraverted, lively communication, lots of ideas. Risk: deep thinking and introvert-friendly tasks (analysis, writing) get crowded out.

**4. The mixed-H team** – diverging <DimBadge code="H" label="Honesty-Humility" /> scores. This is the hardest dynamic. One low-H member in a high-H team can cause a lasting values conflict.

**5. The balanced team** – the ideal is not homogeneity but deliberate heterogeneity. Different profiles can complement each other – if the team is aware of the differences.

## What to look for first in the heatmap

<KeyInsight>
Don't look at the averages – look at the outliers. A person with an extreme score on one dimension (top or bottom 10%) has a bigger effect on team dynamics than all the mid-range members combined.
</KeyInsight>

**An outlier low <DimBadge code="H" label="Honesty-Humility" />** – watch for politicking and information hoarding. This is not a character judgement but an operating style – it just needs to be made conscious.

**An outlier high <DimBadge code="E" label="Emotional stability" /> (high score = low neuroticism)** – this person probably reacts little to stress. Valuable in a crisis; but watch that they don't override others' emotional needs.

**An outlier low <DimBadge code="X" label="Extraversion" />** – this person probably says little in team settings. That doesn't mean less competence – but it has to be considered when designing meeting formats.

## The heatmap as a development tool

The most valuable use is not "exposing" each other – it is interpreting the patterns together. A one-hour team workshop where people ask each other questions based on the heatmap creates more value than any individual coaching session.

The question is not "what is each person like", but: "how do we work together with these differences?"
