The leader of a six-person development team at a mid-sized services company sat across from me in front of the projector. The first page of the team picture showed the aggregate personality picture, the second the roles, the third the trust network and the psychological safety index. She asked: "What am I supposed to see here?" This article answers that question. The story is an anonymised case assembled from several real situations.

## What the leader sees, and what she does not

Start with what is not in the report. There are no named individual results. The leader has no access to members' per-dimension profiles. She sees who has completed the assessment, and she sees the aggregated team picture approved by the consultant. The results of the anonymous measures appear only aggregated, from at least three answers.

This boundary is not an inconvenience. It is what makes it possible for the team to answer honestly. If members know the leader cannot see individual answers, they give real answers to the trust and safety questions. A report a team fills in out of fear is worth nothing.

What the leader does see is three layers. The aggregate personality picture shows where the team's average sits on the six dimensions and how wide the internal diversity is. The team-role map shows which contributions dominate and which are missing. The trust network and the psychological safety index show how well the fabric holds the team together.

## First reading: where does the friction come from?

On the six-person team's picture, the Conscientiousness band was the widest. This means the team had, at the same time, people who put order and deadlines above everything, and people who shape the work flexibly as they go. The leader recognised it immediately. "That is the argument at the weekly status meeting," she said.

<Callout>
The team picture does not say who stands on which side. It says the team has an axis along which people regularly talk past each other. That is enough to move the conversation from the person to the way of working.
</Callout>

On the team-role map, the Quality Guardian and Executor roles dominated, while Idea Generator and Opportunity Scout were under-represented. The leader paused here. The team's task was to renew an ageing product. A strong delivery team had been given an innovation task, and nobody had said out loud that the roles were not set up for it.

The trust network, by contrast, was dense, and the psychological safety index from above-threshold answers was high. So the team trusted each other and dared to argue. The argument just was not pointed at where the problem was.

## What research says about the leader's role

Edmondson's 1999 study examined 51 teams at a manufacturing company. She found that psychological safety is related to team learning behaviour, and through that to performance. In her model, leader behaviour was one source of that safety. In a team with a high index, the leader is already doing many things well, even if she did not know it about herself before the report.

Kozlowski and Ilgen's 2006 review of team-level interventions highlighted that feedback given to a team works when it is about the process rather than individuals, and when the team interprets it itself. Hackman and Wageman, in 2005, studied the timing of leader interventions. A conversation about the way of working is most useful mid-cycle, about goals at the start, and about lessons at the end.

One thing follows from this. The team picture is not the leader's tool against the team. It is the team's tool, together with the leader. That is why the leader does not receive the picture alone. The consultant interprets it together with the team, and the leader is a participant in that.

## The next step: one, not five

The most common mistake I see with the picture is too many lessons. After a report the leader writes down five action points, and three months later none has happened. That is why, at the end of the shared interpretation, we ask one question: what is the one thing the team will do differently next month?

For the six-person team it became this. For the innovation phase they bring in an external Idea Generator from another team, for two sprints. And at the status meeting they make the deadline debate a separate agenda item, so it does not get tangled up in the task debate.

These are not big steps. They are measurable. At the second measurement, the team-role map will show whether the role distribution changed with the addition, and the psychological safety index whether separating the debate harmed trust.

## Back in front of the projector

At the end of the conversation the leader looked at the first page differently. She saw the same wide Conscientiousness band. She just no longer saw a fault to be corrected. She saw two working styles that need separate time. "What am I supposed to see here?" she had asked at the start. The answer: it was not the team she needed to see. It was the axis along which the team talks, and the fact that this can be talked about together.

## Sources and limitations

The team picture shows aggregate patterns without individual rows. It is not suitable for explaining a specific team member's behaviour or for predicting the team's future performance. The anonymised case is a composite of several real situations, with details altered to protect those involved.
