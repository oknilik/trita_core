Matthew Bidwell's 2011 study analysed several years of personnel data from a US financial services firm. Those who came into the same role from outside performed worse in the first two years, left more often, and initially earned about 18 percent more than those promoted from within. This is the result of one organisation in one sector. It serves as a useful warning that external experience does not on its own remove the uncertainty of whether someone will work out.

## An internal candidate brings a different risk

In external hiring, an organisation tries to infer how someone will work from interviews, references, and short selection exercises. That is little information for a long-term decision. In an internal move there is already shared experience, the organisational frame is known, and the person's network does not start from zero.

Internal moves have their own risks, though. Someone will be missing from their current team. The expectations of the old role often stick to the person. Relationships with managers get rearranged too.

<Callout>
Internal mobility is a retention opportunity when it offers a real choice and a path for development. As a forced transfer it produces the opposite effect.
</Callout>

The 2024 review by Ray and colleagues organised more than two hundred studies and found different consequences depending on the form of internal movement. Voluntariness is not a formality here.

## Four sources of information for a mobility decision

A personality or team role profile can help articulate the kind of task and cooperation in which someone feels more at home. It is not suitable for deciding on its own who to move. You need at least four sources.

1. **The person's intent.** A change is only mobility if there is meaningful voluntariness in it.
2. **Demonstrated skills and learning needs.** Current performance is important data. It is not the same as expected performance in another role.
3. **The receiving team's work and relationships.** A new role calls for professional and social settling-in alike.
4. **Observation from several perspectives.** Self-image, the manager's experience, and colleagues' feedback together give a more nuanced starting point.

## What can trita's team picture be used for?

We do not rank internal applications. Team roles and the personality profile can be used as a map for conversation. They may reveal unused contributions, recurring role tensions, or working conditions worth discussing separately.

Three cautious signals can be useful:

- someone consistently takes on tasks that are not part of their official role;
- there is a consistent, interpretable difference between self-image and colleagues' observations;
- an important way of working is concentrated in a single person in the team.

The third signal is the most interesting. If only one person carries the flagging of risks or the difficult conversations with clients, then both cover and development are fragile. This is not an instruction to move anyone. It is a hypothesis to be checked with the person concerned and with their manager.

<KeyInsight>
It does not follow from a measurement that this person should be moved. What follows is that the possibility is worth discussing with them.
</KeyInsight>

## A smaller, reversible trial

Before a permanent transfer, a time-bounded project, a part-time extension of the role, or a mentored rotation is often better. It is cheaper learning for both sides. Stepping back is easier too.

Four things are worth settling in advance. What will the person learn? What support will they get? What will show that it is working? How can they return without losing face if the trial does not work out?

This way internal mobility becomes one further, transparent option. Its value comes from better information, the involvement of the person concerned, and a learning process that can be followed.

## What to look at after the trial

A trial is worth something if a real decision comes at the end of it. That means knowing in advance what you will look at. Impressions alone are not enough, because over a few weeks both enthusiasm and fatigue distort the picture.

Three questions are usually enough. Did the person do the tasks the new role actually contains? What does the receiving team say about the daily cooperation? What do they themselves say about what it was like to work in that role?

The third question is the most important, and the most often skipped. A role can be a poor fit even when the person performs well in it. From a retention point of view, that difference decides.

## What to say to the current manager

The most common obstacle to internal movement is not the receiving side. The current manager loses a person, and often quietly slows the process down for that reason. This situation is worth naming out loud.

Two things help. One is planning the replacement in good time, before the trial. The other is for the organisation to recognise developing people, not only retaining them. Where an internal move counts as a loss, there will be no internal mobility.

## Limitations and sources

Bidwell's result is based on the data of a single organisation in a single sector. It cannot be transferred directly to a Hungarian SME context. The 18 percent pay difference is not a general rule either, but a characteristic of that sample. The advantages of internal mobility are worth checking against your own organisation's data.
