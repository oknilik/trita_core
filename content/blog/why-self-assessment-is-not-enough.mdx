## The same person, from two vantage points

We know things about ourselves that nobody else can see: our intentions, our doubts, the background to a situation. The people around us, in turn, see the part of this that shows up in our shared moments. Both pictures are real - they are just taken from different positions.

This is the duality the Johari window describes: there is what we know about ourselves and others see too, what we keep to ourselves, and what is only visible from the outside. Comparing the two perspectives is what makes those open and hidden areas visible.

<Callout>
Self-report and the feedback of the people around us do not compete. They show the same person from two vantage points, and together they see more than either does alone.
</Callout>

According to the 2010 meta-analysis by Connelly and Ones, for several traits the ratings of people who know us well track actual behaviour at least as closely as self-report does. Relying on self-assessment alone leaves out an important perspective.

## The difference is the most valuable signal

When the two pictures line up, that is confirmation: what someone believes about themselves is what others experience. The most useful conversations, however, start where the pictures differ. Three typical patterns:

**Hidden strength.** The people around someone rate their calm or reliability higher than they rate it themselves. Think of the person who feels they are “just doing their job” - while everyone turns to them whenever something breaks. Spoken out loud, this becomes something to build on; unspoken, it may stay invisible.

**Blind spot.** They experience themselves as always quick to reply - while others often wait days to hear back. Neither of them is wrong: one knows their intention, the others know what actually reached them. That difference is exactly what is worth talking about.

**Different situations.** Someone who only meets you in meetings sees something different from someone who also knows how you work when you are deep in a task on your own. The two pictures can differ while both are accurate - they simply describe different situations, which is why it is worth asking who is seeing this from where.

## Why can the two views differ?

The gap has everyday causes, even without any bias:

- **We see different situations.** We know ourselves across many contexts; the other person mainly knows the shared ones.
- **Not everything is visible from outside.** Social signals of <DimBadge code="X" label="Extraversion" /> are easy to observe; private deliberation or anxiety much less so.
- **How well someone knows you matters.** In the 2024 study by Lee, de Vries and Ashton, covering 5,405 people, the two pictures tended to agree more closely in closer relationships.
- **Measurement always carries uncertainty.** How items are read, and what is going on in someone's life, both shape the answers.
- **An ideal self may play a part.** Sometimes we partly describe the person we would like to be.

## Three questions for your next conversation

<KeyInsight>
A difference does not necessarily mean someone is wrong. It is far more often a sign that there is something here worth talking about.
</KeyInsight>

When a comparison shows a larger gap, these three questions almost always help uncover the reason:

1. **“In which situations do we see each other?”** The picture may cover only a few recurring situations, while most of life happens elsewhere.
2. **“What exactly do we expect from each other?”** On the fast-thorough or independent-aligned scale, differing expectations often show up as differing ratings.
3. **“Is there a side of you you feel we don't see?”** This is where hidden strengths tend to surface.

## Who should you invite?

The people you invite decide what you get to see. Three to five is usually enough. Include someone who sees your everyday life, and someone who only meets you in a few recurring situations.

Don't invite only the people you get along with easily. The most informative feedback often comes from someone you occasionally find harder to be around. An old acquaintance and a new one will give you different pictures - and that difference is information in itself.

## Sources and limitations

If you see a difference here and there, that on its own proves nothing: neither that you see yourself in a distorted way, nor that you have changed. Large-sample research found no pattern of us generally seeing ourselves as better or worse than we are. Which way the two pictures diverge varies from person to person and from relationship to relationship. And smaller differences often sit within the natural spread of the measurement itself. It is concrete situations that give a difference its meaning - on its own, it is not worth labelling anyone with.
