A team role is not a personality trait. It is behaviour your teammates see in the shared work. That is why trita's team-role questionnaire asks the same twenty-seven statements from two perspectives: to you about yourself, and to your teammates about you. The difference between the two pictures is the most valuable result.

## What do we mean by a team role?

We call a team role the contribution someone naturally brings to shared work. Some people come up with a new approach when the team is stuck. Others keep deadlines and details under control. Neither is a good or bad trait. A team needs both, at different moments.

The Trita team-role model distinguishes nine roles: Idea Generator, Opportunity Scout, Coordinator, Driver, Critical Evaluator, Team Supporter, Executor, Quality Guardian and Domain Expert. Each role has three observable behaviours. One of the Quality Guardian statements reads: "I spot the errors that escaped others' attention."

Role research has long recognised that role and personality are related but not the same. Stewart, Fulmer and Barrick, in a 2005 study, described role behaviour as a mediating level between individual traits and team outcomes. A trait gives an inclination. The role is what actually materialises from it in this particular team.

## How does completion work?

The respondent selects the eight to twelve statements that fit best out of the twenty-seven. From those, exactly three are marked as especially characteristic. A highlighted statement counts double. This structure is deliberate. You cannot attribute everything to everyone, so the picture becomes sharper.

<Callout>
The forced-choice format enforces what happens in everyday work anyway: we are not strong at everything at once. The picture becomes useful because it ranks.
</Callout>

Scoring is simple. For each role we add up the weights of the selected statements and relate the sum to the theoretical maximum. Each role therefore receives a value between 0 and 100. The three highest values form the primary role picture.

In the peer version the same statements are phrased in the third person. "Spots the errors that escaped others' attention." Teammates complete it about one person, each on their own. The aggregate picture is the average of the per-rater role profiles. It appears only from at least three raters' answers, and nobody sees who selected what.

## What does the peer perspective add?

Self-image on its own can be distorted for two reasons. We select what we would like to believe about ourselves. And we cannot see how our behaviour lands on others. Mathieu and colleagues validated a six-role team-role instrument in 2015. They found that self-reported role orientation is related to how teammates rate actual role behaviour, but the two pictures do not coincide.

That difference is the core of our product. If someone sees themselves as a Coordinator and their team sees a Driver, that is not a measurement error. It is a conversation starter. In the consultant-led interpretation, three questions come up: where do the two pictures agree, where do they diverge, and what does the team do with that.

Mumford and colleagues approached from another direction in 2008. They measured team-role knowledge, meaning whether someone recognises which situation calls for which role. This knowledge was associated with the performance ratings teammates gave. A role, then, is not only an inclination. It is also situational judgement.

## What does the questionnaire not measure?

It does not measure ability. If a team sees someone as a Domain Expert, we still do not know how good they are at their profession. The questionnaire shows what contribution is expected of them and what they take on.

It does not give a permanent label. The role picture can change from team to team. The same person may be a Critical Evaluator in an experienced team and a Driver in a new one, because that is what is needed there. We therefore always tie the picture to the specific team and do not carry it elsewhere.

It does not rank people. The nine roles are equal. A team has no "ideal" role distribution that could be read off a table. Composition depends on the task, and a missing role can often be covered by deliberate division of labour.

## What happens when there is no peer data yet?

In that case trita estimates the likely roles from the personality profile. We always show this estimate with a source label. The interface makes it visible that you are looking at an estimated picture, and that measured data requires peer completion. As soon as there are at least three raters, the measured picture overrides the estimate.

This source labelling is a core principle of the product. We never show estimated and measured data the same way.

## Sources and limitations

The Trita team-role model and the twenty-seven-statement questionnaire are our own development. The research cited supports the measurability of role behaviour and the difference between perspectives, not our specific instrument. Validation of the instrument is ongoing, and results are shown in the interface with source and confidence labels.

The team-role picture is one layer of the team picture. What it shows together with the other layers, the trust network and psychological safety, is summarised on our [team diagnostics page](/team-dynamics). If you are interested in reading the aggregate personality picture, start with our [article on the team's personality picture](/blog/reading-your-teams-personality-picture).

*A note on the references: the three studies use different role taxonomies from the Trita team-role model. The number and names of roles differ by instrument. The shared lesson concerns how to measure, not the specific list of roles.*
